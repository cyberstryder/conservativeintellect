Departing Man Was an "MS-13 Affiliated, Wife Beating, Human Trafficker," Commentator Announces; the Record, Checked at the Gate, Shows Zero Convictions, Zero Gang Charges, and a Smuggling Case a Federal Judge Threw Out as Vindictive

By Biff Carrington November 25, 2026 5 min read
✈

WASHINGTON — Kilmar Abrego Garcia "has officially SELF DEPORTED," a commentator announced Wednesday, and on that much, for once, the announcement and the record agree: on Tuesday night he walked onto a commercial flight at Washington Dulles, in a seat he had effectively chosen, bound for a country he had chosen, with his ankle monitor removed at the gate and his wife beside him. It is the middle of the post — the part where he is introduced as "an MS-13 affiliated, wife beating, human trafficker" — where the record gets off the plane.

"Democrats sipped margaritas with and defended an MS-13 affiliated, wife beating, human trafficker who was in this country illegally," wrote Benny Johnson. Three labels, no verbs wasted on "alleged." So let us do the paperwork the post skipped.

Start with the whole criminal record, which is short enough to read aloud before takeoff: Kilmar Abrego Garcia has never been convicted of a crime. Not in the United States, not in El Salvador, not anywhere. He has also never been charged with gang membership. That is not his lawyer's summary; it is USA Today's, published today, in the same article that confirms the departure Johnson was celebrating.

The MS-13 label traces to a single 2019 county police report, written after Abrego Garcia was picked up outside a Home Depot in Maryland. The report cited a confidential informant who said he belonged to an MS-13 clique in New York — a state he had never lived in. He denied it then and denies it now. The detective who filed the report was later fired over an unrelated misconduct case. No gang charge was ever filed, by anyone, in the six years since. "Affiliated," in the post, is doing the work of a conviction that does not exist, on the strength of an informant's claim about a clique in a state the man had never lived in.

The "human trafficker" label had its day in court, which is more than the other two got. When the administration returned Abrego Garcia to the United States in June 2025 — after conceding his first deportation to El Salvador was an administrative error carried out despite a judge's order — it charged him with two human-smuggling counts from a 2022 Tennessee traffic stop. On May 22, 2026, U.S. District Judge Waverly Crenshaw dismissed the case, finding the prosecution vindictive: Homeland Security had known about the traffic stop for two years, had closed its investigation, and reopened it only after Abrego Garcia won his lawsuit over the wrongful deportation. "The evidence before this Court sadly reflects an abuse of prosecuting power," the judge wrote. The Justice Department has appealed the dismissal — appeals are noted honestly in this newspaper — but an appealed dismissal is still a dismissal, and a dismissed charge is not a job title.

The third label deserves the most care, because the underlying allegations are serious and were made under oath. In 2020 and 2021, Jennifer Vasquez Sura filed civil petitions for protective orders describing incidents of abuse. Those petitions are real documents, and this newspaper does not make sport of them. What they are not is a criminal record: no charges were ever filed over them, no court ever adjudicated them, and the petitions were dismissed when she did not appear at the follow-up hearings. Vasquez Sura later said she had acted out of an abundance of caution after surviving domestic violence in a previous relationship, that the couple worked through their problems privately, including counseling — and whatever one makes of that, the woman the post calls beaten spent Tuesday night on a plane to Madrid at her husband's side, having sued the United States government to bring him home. "Wife beating," stated as a flat identity, converts unadjudicated civil allegations into a verdict no court issued.

"We keep the labels in a drawer with the verdicts," said Dale Krebbs, 54, a fictional local man who labels his leftovers with dates, unlike commentators. "If there's no verdict in the drawer, I don't get to write the label. I checked on the plane man. The drawer is empty. There's a dismissal in there, though — that one's got a judge's signature and everything."

None of this requires admiring Abrego Garcia, and the true parts of the post are genuinely true. He entered the United States illegally as a teenager around 2011 — though an immigration judge's 2019 withholding-of-removal order then let him live and work here legally, checking in with ICE as required, which is why his 2025 deportation to El Salvador, carried out in defiance of that order, was conceded to be an error. He was ordered back, he was brought back, and this week he left, on terms a federal judge set: his choice of country, his family beside him, no shackles, no ICE Air flight to Liberia. The administration that spent twenty months trying to send him to Africa in chains is entitled to call his chosen exit a win. It is even, in the narrow sense, a self-deportation — Judge Xinis's order says the departure counts as execution of his removal order.

What it is not is a conviction. A man can be removable without being a trafficker, deportable without being a gang member, and gone without any of the three labels in the announcement having survived contact with a courtroom. The post needed a villain with a résumé. The record supplied a sheet-metal apprentice with an empty convictions column and a dismissed indictment. The résumé was printed anyway.

At press time, the convictions column remained at zero, the gang-charges column remained at zero, the one case that reached a judge remained dismissed by one, and the labels had been located where they have always lived — in the announcement, which is not a court, no matter how many capital letters it uses.

Actually True

Abrego Garcia has never been convicted of a crime and never been charged with gang membership. USA Today (Nov. 25, 2026): "Abrego Garcia has never been convicted of a crime or charged with gang membership. The only criminal charges filed against him, the Tennessee smuggling counts, were dismissed this spring by a federal judge who found the prosecution vindictive."

The only charges ever filed — the Tennessee human-smuggling counts — were dismissed on May 22, 2026. Judge Waverly Crenshaw found the prosecution vindictive and tainted: the 2022 traffic-stop investigation had been closed and was reopened only after Abrego Garcia won his wrongful-deportation lawsuit. "The evidence before this Court sadly reflects an abuse of prosecuting power" (CNN; The Tennessean, May 22, 2026). The Justice Department appealed the dismissal in August 2026; an appealed dismissal is still not a conviction.

The MS-13 allegation rests on one 2019 police report and an informant's claim about a state he never lived in. County police, citing a confidential informant, accused him of belonging to an MS-13 clique in New York; he had never lived in New York, denied the allegation, was never charged, and the detective who filed the report was later fired over an unrelated misconduct case (USA Today, Nov. 25, 2026).

The abuse allegations were civil petitions, never criminal charges. His wife's 2020–2021 protective-order petitions alleged physical abuse; none resulted in criminal charges, and the petitions were dismissed when she did not appear at follow-up hearings. She later said she acted out of caution after a prior abusive relationship, that the couple went to counseling, and she has publicly stood by him — she traveled with him to Madrid this week (The Dispatch; CNN; USA Today).

What is true in the original post: He did leave voluntarily — "self-deported" is the administration's own term, and DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin used it — flying commercially from Dulles on Nov. 24 to Spain, the country he chose, under Judge Paula Xinis's Nov. 20 order; the order specifies the departure counts as execution of his removal order. He also did enter the U.S. illegally as a teenager (~2011) — though a 2019 withholding-of-removal order let him live and work here legally thereafter, which is why the government conceded his March 2025 deportation to El Salvador was an "administrative error."
