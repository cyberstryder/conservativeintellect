Cutting USAID Elected Seven Right-Wing Presidents, Influencer Announces; Bolivia Expelled USAID in 2013, and the Chile and Costa Rica Offices Closed in 1996

By Chip Hollister November 25, 2026 6 min read
💸

WASHINGTON — The USAID files just dropped, an influencer announced Wednesday, and they show two things: that Democrats spent billions of tax dollars censoring free speech, and that the moment the money stopped, right-wing candidates won in Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Honduras, and Peru.

"The USAID files just dropped and it shows Democrats spent billions of our tax dollars on censoring free speech," wrote DC_Draino. "After USAID funding stopped, right wing candidates have won in Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Honduras, and Peru."

Begin with the part that is true, because it is the load-bearing part: the list. Ecuador re-elected Daniel Noboa in April 2025. Bolivia's October 2025 runoff ended nearly twenty years of socialist MAS rule. Chile chose José Antonio Kast in December. Honduras went for Nasry Asfura. Costa Rica, Peru, and Colombia followed in 2026. Seven elections, seven right-leaning winners. A fact-check of the streak graded the list itself consistent with the record. The streak is real. It is the explanation that has the problem, and the problem has a date — three dates, actually.

Bolivia expelled USAID in 2013. USAID closed its missions in Chile and Costa Rica in 1996. This is the finding of the Hudson Institute's Gil Guerra, a conservative analyst who studied the USAID theory precisely because his side was circulating it, tracing the money award by award through the government's own spending databases. Three of the seven exhibits in Wednesday's post come from countries where there was no USAID funding left to stop — in Bolivia's case for thirteen years before the freeze, in Chile's and Costa Rica's for twenty-nine. A funding cut cannot take credit for an election in a country the agency left during the Clinton administration, any more than turning off a faucet can dry up a house that was never plumbed.

The pattern in the countries USAID was funding runs the wrong way, too. Guerra measured it the obvious way: if USAID money was propping up the left, the left should have collapsed hardest where the most money was cut. In the countries where USAID spent $3 to $13 per person a year, the left's vote share fell about nine points — about two, once Honduras and its imploded left coalition are set aside. In the countries where USAID spent under 35 cents a person, the left's share fell by an average of nineteen points. Bolivia, which threw the agency out in 2013, recorded the biggest left collapse of all. The dose and the response point in opposite directions.

Then there are the billions. The files released this week — some 248 records, heavy on guidebooks, meeting notes, and one remarkable 2022 seminar in which a Global Disinformation Index cofounder pitched USAID on defunding, downranking, and deplatforming unwanted speech, on the theory that disinformation is "NOT true or false" — are a genuine story about a government agency entertaining a censorship-adjacent sales pitch. What they are not is a ledger showing billions spent censoring Americans. An independent tally of the official ForeignAssistance.gov data finds roughly $24.6 million in programs explicitly labelled disinformation across the usable years, and about $359 million for media and free-flow-of-information programs of every kind over six years, out of roughly $150.6 billion in total USAID obligations. The Hudson study, working the Latin America numbers separately, found that the categories of spending most likely to touch elections or media came to about a penny of every USAID dollar in the five mission countries — $2.9 billion obligated across five countries and five years for everything, food, medicine, migration relief, and all. "Billions on censoring free speech" takes the agency's entire regional budget, renames all of it censorship, and rounds up.

"We stopped paying for sunshine in 1996 and the sun kept coming up, so obviously the payments were rigged," said Dale Fenn, 61, a fictional retired geography teacher who has been crediting and blaming absent agencies for weather in three hemispheres. "Then we stopped paying in countries we'd never been in at all, and it rained victories. Coincidence is just causation that hasn't hired a publicist."

There is a simpler explanation on the record, and Guerra — again, a conservative, writing for a conservative institute, about results he says ranged for him from relief to enthusiasm — lands on it: Latin American voters fire incumbents. From 2018 to 2022, incumbent parties lost 16 of 18 presidential contests, most of the losers then being on the right; the left-wing replacements then got fired in the next wave, which happened to coincide with the USAID freeze. Even the streak's edges resist the theory. In Peru, Keiko Fujimori lost the vote cast inside Peru and won on ballots cast by Peruvians living abroad — the electorate least exposed to USAID. And in Brazil, the largest country on the continent, the left's candidate beat Flávio Bolsonaro in October — a result Wednesday's companion post handles by declaring, without evidence offered, that "the election was stolen in Brazil."

None of this requires anyone to defend USAID's disinformation portfolio, which the released files make legitimately uncomfortable reading, or to begrudge anyone their enjoyment of the Latin American results. It merely observes the distance between a list and a ledger. The list is accurate. The ledger says the money was smaller, older, and in three countries entirely absent — and that the left fell hardest exactly where the supposed prop was thinnest. A correlation that survives only in the countries where its cause doesn't exist is not a smoking gun. It is a coincidence with a marketing department.

At press time, USAID's Chile office, closed since 1996, had declined to comment on its decisive role in the 2025 Chilean election, as had its Costa Rica office, and Bolivia's had been reached only by travelling back to before its 2013 expulsion, where it was busy not influencing the 2025 election either.

Actually True

The seven elections are real. Right-leaning candidates won the presidential elections in Ecuador (Daniel Noboa, re-elected April 2025, 55.63% in the runoff), Bolivia (Rodrigo Paz, October 2025, ending nearly 20 years of MAS rule), Chile (José Antonio Kast, Dec. 14 runoff, 58.2%–41.8%), Honduras (Nasry Asfura, 40.27%), Costa Rica (Laura Fernández, Feb. 1, 2026, 48.53%), Peru (Keiko Fujimori, June 7, 2026 runoff, 50.135%), and Colombia (Abelardo de la Espriella, by about one point, June 2026). A Polygraph fact-check of the streak graded the list itself supported; analysts dispute only the cause.

Three of the seven countries had no USAID mission to cut. Hudson Institute (Gil Guerra, "Who's Afraid of USAID?", Oct. 29, 2026), tracing USAspending.gov and ForeignAssistance.gov records: of the eight countries that voted for president after the freeze, only five still hosted USAID missions (Colombia, Honduras, Peru, Ecuador, Brazil). Bolivia expelled USAID in 2013; the Chile and Costa Rica missions closed in 1996.

Where USAID spent more, the left fell less — the opposite of the theory. Hudson: in countries receiving $3–$13 per person per year, the left's vote share fell an average of nine points (about two excluding Honduras); in countries under 35 cents per person, it fell an average of nineteen points. Bolivia, with no USAID since 2013, saw the largest left decline of any country studied.

The money that could touch elections or media was about a penny on the dollar. Hudson: USAID obligated $2.9 billion to the five mission countries across FY2020–2024 for all purposes; about 56 cents of each dollar went to American contractors and nonprofits, 29 cents to the UN and multilateral agencies (much of it food aid for Venezuelan migrants), about eight cents directly to local organizations — and the rights, advocacy, watchdog, and media categories together received about one cent per dollar.

No tally gets "censoring" to billions. An independent audit of ForeignAssistance.gov obligations (FY2020–2026 usable extracts) finds roughly $24.6 million in programs explicitly labelled disinformation, about $359 million for media and free-flow-of-information programs overall, and about $2.5 billion across seven broad political-and-civic categories worldwide — out of roughly $150.6 billion in total USAID obligations. A separate review of the released files and spending records found no USAID dollar shown to have paid for a U.S. campaign, a U.S. candidate, or a named U.S. content-moderation decision.

What the USAID files actually show: the records released Nov. 24–25, 2026 (about 248 files) include disinformation guidebooks, communications with Google and Meta, and a 2022 seminar at which Global Disinformation Index cofounder Danny Rogers urged USAID to defund, downrank, and deplatform unwanted speech and said disinformation is "NOT true or false." A vendor's pitch at a seminar is documented; "billions spent censoring free speech" is not.

The streak has an ending the post omits, and an explanation it skips. In Brazil, the left's candidate defeated Flávio Bolsonaro in October 2026 (the companion post calls that election "stolen," offering no evidence). And from 2018 to 2022 incumbent parties lost 16 of 18 Latin American presidential contests — anti-incumbency, not aid flows, is the pattern that covers both waves.

What is true in the original post: the seven winners, the fact of the USAID shutdown (spending frozen Jan. 20, 2025; 83% of programs cancelled March 10; remaining operations transferred to the State Department July 1, 2025), and the existence of the released files. The billions and the causation are the false parts, and they are the parts that make the post a story.
