Court Blocks Both of Virginia's ICE Laws, Internet Announces; Court, Checking Its Own Ruling, Finds It Blocked One and Let Virginia Enforce the Other

By Chip Hollister November 23, 2026 4 min read
⚖

RICHMOND — A federal appeals court blocked Virginia's ICE laws on Monday, the internet announced, in a ruling the court itself described somewhat differently: it blocked one law and cleared Virginia to enforce the other one, in the same opinion, on the same day, in paragraphs anyone could read in either order.

“BREAKING: A federal appeals court just BLOCKED a Virginia law that restricts local police from cooperating with ICE and bans ICE from wearing masks,” wrote Libs of TikTok, combining two separate statutes, signed two months apart, into a single law and a single outcome.

The two laws had two different fates. On the cooperation law — the one limiting when Virginia's state and local agencies may sign or keep 287(g) agreements deputizing local officers for federal immigration work — the Fourth Circuit vacated the injunction that had been protecting those agreements. Virginia may now enforce its new terms or end the agreements. That is the legal opposite of blocked: the restriction the post says a court stopped is the restriction a court just allowed.

On the mask law — the one barring on-duty officers, federal ones included, from covering their faces and requiring visible identification — the panel did the other thing. It affirmed the injunction against enforcing that law on federal officers, finding it likely violates the Constitution because a state may not directly regulate federal operations. One law blocked, one law unblocked, one post reporting both as blocked.

“A split decision is a decision that splits,” said Dale Krebbs, 54, a local man who reads court opinions exclusively through breaking-news posts. “If half of it goes one way and half goes the other, and you report the whole thing as your half, you have not summarized the ruling. You have taken it home, cut it in half, and kept the half you liked.”

The details the merger erased are not footnotes. Judge Toby Heytens, a Biden appointee, wrote the opinion. The panel held the cooperation law “neither directly regulates the Federal Government nor prevents private parties from contracting with it,” and noted every existing agreement expressly lets the non-federal party terminate without cause — which is a difficult position from which to argue Virginia was forbidden to terminate them. Judge Robert King, a Clinton appointee, agreed on cooperation and dissented on masks, arguing he would have let Virginia enforce the mask law too — meaning the court's most mask-friendly judge still voted to let the cooperation restrictions stand.

None of this requires liking either law. A reader may cheer the mask ruling and curse the cooperation ruling, or the reverse, and this newspaper takes no position on which emotion to schedule first. It merely notes that a ruling in which Virginia won the power to limit ICE cooperation cannot be honestly filed under “court blocks Virginia's limits on ICE cooperation,” unless the filing system is a hat.

At press time, the cooperation restrictions remained enforceable, the mask ban remained blocked, and the post remained uncorrected — three facts, of which the internet had reported one.

Actually True

  • There were two laws, not one, and they were decided opposite ways. Virginia's 287(g) cooperation law (Va. Code § 15.2-1726.1, signed April 22, 2026) limits state/local agreements to help ICE. Its mask/identification law (Va. Code § 19.2-83.6:2, signed May 20, 2026) bars on-duty officers from wearing facial coverings and requires visible ID, with exceptions for tactical, undercover, and protective uses. The Fourth Circuit decided both appeals together in United States v. Virginia, Nos. 26-01874 and 26-2258, on November 23, 2026.
  • The cooperation restrictions were NOT blocked — the court let Virginia enforce them. The panel vacated the preliminary injunction that had barred Virginia from rendering existing 287(g) agreements unenforceable, holding the law does not violate intergovernmental immunity (it “neither directly regulates the Federal Government nor prevents private parties from contracting with it”) and does not violate the Contracts Clause, because the agreements expressly permit unilateral termination without cause.
  • Only the mask/ID law was blocked as to federal officers. The panel affirmed the preliminary injunction against enforcing the mask/identification law on federal officers, holding it likely violates intergovernmental immunity because it directly regulates federal employees performing federal duties. Judge Robert B. King concurred on the cooperation ruling and dissented on masks — he would have vacated the mask injunction as well.
  • Both rulings are preliminary, not final. Each decides likelihood of success on the merits at the injunction stage; the underlying case returns to the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia (No. 3:26-cv-00545-REP) for a decision on the merits.

Sources: Bloomberg Law, “Virginia Can't Enforce ICE Mask Ban, Fourth Circuit Says” (Nov. 23, 2026); Reason / The Volokh Conspiracy, “Fourth Circuit Upholds Virginia's Law Terminating Agreements to Help With Immigration Enforcement — but Strikes Down Anti-Mask Law” (Nov. 23, 2026); TML News, “Fourth Circuit Blocks Virginia Mask Ban For Federal Officers” (Nov. 23, 2026).
