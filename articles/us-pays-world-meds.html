America Pays for All the World's Medicine and All Its Research, Internet Announces; Europe, Which Spent €56 Billion on Drug Research Last Year, Is Surprised to Learn It Paid Nothing

By Chip Hollister November 23, 2026 4 min read
⚕

Washington — The United States is single-handedly paying for the entire planet’s prescription drugs and every scrap of medical research behind them, the internet announced Monday, in a finding that came as startling news to the 33 other wealthy nations that believed they had been buying their own medicine all along.

“You also have to factor in the us subsidizing prescription meds for the rest of the world,” wrote one health economist on Reddit’s r/Conservative, in the day’s most popular thread. If America stopped, foreign governments “may have to actually start charging their citizens for meds instead of the us. That would ruin their entire ‘free’ Healthcare system and collapse their economy so that could just not happen.”

“We pay for all the med research,” the economist added, in a follow-up the British, German, Swiss, Japanese, French, and Chinese research establishments read with the particular sadness of people being informed they do not exist.

The kernel of truth, analysts conceded, is real and it is not small: Americans do pay far more for the same drugs. A RAND study prepared for the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services found U.S. prices in 2022 ran 2.78 times those in 33 other OECD countries, and 4.22 times for brand-name drugs. “That part is true, and it’s genuinely outrageous,” said Dale Krebbs, 54, a local man who reads drug-pricing studies exclusively through comment sections. “But paying Pfizer too much for your own prescription is not the same as paying for a stranger’s prescription in Lyon. One of those is a price. The other would be a check. Nobody has ever shown anybody the check.”

“‘All’ is a strong word. It is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It is, in fact, doing €56 billion of work, in Europe alone.”

The research claim collapsed first. Europe’s pharmaceutical industry alone spent €56.3 billion on research and development in 2024 — €11 billion of it in Britain, €10.7 billion in Germany, €9.6 billion in Switzerland, home to Roche and Novartis, two companies apparently unaware their research budgets were being covered from Ohio. “All,” Krebbs noted, “is a strong word. It is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It is, in fact, doing €56 billion of work, in Europe alone, before Japan and China are even allowed into the room.”

The collapse prediction fared no better. Britain’s National Health Service has been funded principally out of British general taxation and National Insurance contributions since British taxpayers invented it on July 5, 1948 — decades before the modern American price gap existed to supposedly prop it up. Foreign systems are not funded by American pharmacy receipts; they are funded the boring way, by their own citizens’ taxes, which is why their citizens describe them, accurately, as systems they pay for.

Even the price story, told whole, refuses to cooperate: the same RAND study found American prices for unbranded generics — about 90% of U.S. prescription volume — are actually lower than abroad, at 67% of the comparison average. The subsidy, it turns out, applies only to the expensive brand-name shelf, which is a strange design for a program allegedly bankrolling every healthcare system on Earth.

At press time, the thread had moved on, secure in the knowledge that if American drug prices ever fell, the economies of 33 nations would collapse — a theory under which the most generous foreign-aid program in history is run, accidentally, by whoever sets the price of insulin in Indiana.

Actually True

The price gap is real — and it’s a price, not a payment to foreigners. RAND, for the HHS Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (published February 2024, using 2022 data), found U.S. prescription prices were 2.78× those in 33 OECD comparison countries across all drugs, and 4.22× for brand-name originator drugs (still over 3× after adjusting for U.S. rebates). Foreign buyers pay the lower negotiated prices themselves, out of their own tax and insurance systems; no U.S. money is transferred to them.

“We pay for all the med research” is false by tens of billions. Europe’s research-based pharmaceutical industry invested €56,344 million (≈€56.3 billion) in R&D in 2024 (EFPIA figures), including €11,023 million in the UK, €10,728 million in Germany, and €9,553 million in Switzerland. That is Europe alone — before Japan, China, or anyone else.

The same study undercuts the subsidy story on generics. RAND found U.S. prices for unbranded generic drugs — roughly 90% of U.S. prescription volume — were about 67% of the comparison-country average, i.e. cheaper in America. The gap lives in brand-name drugs.

Foreign “free” systems are funded by foreign taxpayers, and long predate the gap. The NHS, founded July 5, 1948, has “since its foundation, been funded principally out of general taxation and national insurance contributions” (House of Lords Library). British healthcare is free at the point of use because Britons are taxed for it — not because Americans overpay for brand-name drugs.

No evidence supports the collapse claim. Nothing in the RAND/ASPE report or EFPIA data suggests any foreign health system depends on U.S. drug prices for its existence; those systems’ budgets come from their own taxation and social-insurance contributions, and would be unchanged if U.S. prices fell — the loss would land on drug-company revenue, not on foreign treasuries.

Sources: RAND Corporation, “International Prescription Drug Price Comparisons: Estimates Using 2022 Data” (for HHS/ASPE, Feb. 2024); EFPIA, “The Pharmaceutical Industry in Figures” / Key Data (2024 R&D investment, €56,344M); House of Lords Library, “The NHS at 75: The past and future of its funding model.”
