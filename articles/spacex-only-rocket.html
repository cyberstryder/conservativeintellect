SpaceX Is the Only Company That Can Launch Things, Internet Declares; Space Force, Which Just Paid Three Companies $13.7 Billion to Launch Things, Is Surprised

By Biff Carrington November 22, 2026 4 min read
🚀

Watch Chip Hollister tell it:

Cape Canaveral, Fla. — America has exactly one company capable of launching rockets, the internet announced Sunday, in a finding that came as startling news to the United States Space Force, which last year signed launch contracts with three of them.

“Two things: SpaceX is the only capable launch provider,” wrote one aerospace analyst on Reddit’s r/Conservative, in a thread about whether SpaceX’s government funding should be cut. “And also government contracts are not that significant to the SpaceX bottom line.”

The second finding was supported by a colleague, who reported that SpaceX “only receives about 106 million in subsidies and tax breaks between state and federal” — a figure roughly 200 times smaller than the $22 billion in government contracts SpaceX’s own president has cited, and one that would make the company’s NASA crew flights, cargo runs, lunar lander, and spy-satellite launches the most generous volunteer work in the history of flight.

The Space Force, reached for comment, pointed to its own paperwork. In April 2025 it awarded its National Security Space Launch Phase 3 Lane 2 contracts — the missions for its most demanding, least risk-tolerant payloads — to SpaceX, United Launch Alliance, and Blue Origin, in that order, at anticipated values of $5.9 billion, $5.4 billion, and $2.4 billion. The manifest splits about 54 missions roughly 60/40 between SpaceX and ULA, with Blue Origin projected for seven more. Officials described this arrangement as “what you do when there is one capable provider, apparently.”

“I’ve launched 18 rockets this year and every single one of them worked,” said no one at Rocket Lab, which was too busy launching its 19th to comment. “Please update the list of capable providers to include us. And ULA. And the Europeans. And also ULA again, because they would like to be counted twice after the week they’ve had.”

“There’s a word for the only one. The word is ‘only.’ It does not mean ‘the best one, plus some other ones the government also paid thirteen billion dollars.’”

Analysts were quick to concede the strongest version of the claim: SpaceX is, by a wide margin, the dominant launch provider on Earth, flies more than everyone else, and lands its boosters on barges like it’s showing off. “Dominant, yes. Cheapest, probably. Only, no,” said Dale Krebbs, 54, a local man who follows the space program exclusively through comment sections. “There’s a word for the only one. The word is ‘only.’ It does not mean ‘the best one, plus some other ones the government also paid thirteen billion dollars.’”

The other providers have admittedly had a rough month — ULA’s Vulcan failed minutes after liftoff on November 15, and Blue Origin’s New Glenn has not flown since a May explosion on the pad — a stretch the internet cited as proof of non-existence, under the long-standing rule that a company whose rocket is being repaired has been deleted from the economy.

At press time, the thread had moved on to its next finding: that a company can simultaneously be the government’s irreplaceable sole provider and barely notice the government’s money, a position experts described as “having it both ways, in orbit.”

Actually True

The Space Force itself hired three “capable” providers. On April 4, 2025, Space Systems Command awarded National Security Space Launch Phase 3 Lane 2 contracts to SpaceX ($5,923,580,297 anticipated), United Launch Services/ULA ($5,366,439,406), and Blue Origin ($2,386,234,812) — about $13.7 billion combined for the nation’s most demanding national-security launches. The projected split of roughly 54 Lane 2 missions (FY25–FY29) is SpaceX 28, ULA 19, Blue Origin 7 (U.S. Space Force / Space Systems Command release, April 4, 2025).

Other providers are flying, successfully, right now. Orbital launches in 2026 to date: SpaceX 126, Rocket Lab 18 at a 100% success rate, ULA 6, Arianespace 4 — SpaceX is dominant, not alone (2026 launch tally, SpaceNexus, updated Nov 2026). ULA’s Vulcan did fail on November 15, 2026, and Blue Origin’s New Glenn is grounded until a targeted January 2027 return — real setbacks, neither of which un-exists either company or its Space Force contract.

Government contracts are very significant to SpaceX. A Washington Post analysis (Feb. 26, 2025) found Elon Musk’s companies had received at least $38 billion in government contracts, loans, subsidies, and tax credits since 2003, with SpaceX alone holding $22 billion in government contracts as of 2024, per SpaceX president Gwynne Shotwell — against the thread’s “about 106 million in subsidies and tax breaks,” which is off by roughly two orders of magnitude even before counting contracts.

Sources: U.S. Space Force, Space Systems Command, “Space Systems Command awards National Security Space Launch Phase 3 Lane 2 contracts” (Apr. 4, 2025); Washington Post, “Elon Musk’s business empire is built on $38 billion in government funding” (Feb. 26, 2025); SpaceNexus, “Rocket Launch Schedule 2026” launch-provider tally (Nov. 2026).
