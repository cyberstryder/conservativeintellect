Newsom Granted 'Clemency & Mercy' to 548 of the Most Evil Monsters, President Announces; the Clemency, Records Show, Sends Every One of Them to Prison for Life Without Parole, Was Announced 19 Days Ago, and 382 of the 548 Still Need a Court's Permission

By Biff Carrington November 24, 2026 4 min read
⛓

Watch Chip Hollister tell it:

WASHINGTON — Just days ago, the President announced Tuesday, California Gov. Gavin Newsom granted clemency and mercy to 548 of the most evil monsters, a spree of leniency that, on inspection of the actual order, condemns every one of the 548 to die in prison.

“Just days ago, the Gov. of California, Gavin Newsom, granted clemency & mercy to 548 of the most EVIL monsters,” the White House posted, quoting President Donald J. Trump. “In the Trump Admin we believe in the exact opposite, we're fighting to uplift the law abiding & protect the most vulnerable.”

The clemency is real, and so is the number. What it does is the part that did not survive the trip to the podium. On November 5 — nineteen days before Tuesday's post, in a month the calendar insists on calling three weeks ago — Newsom announced he would commute all of California's death sentences to life in prison without the possibility of parole. Not pardons. Not releases. Not mercy of the kind that opens a gate. Every one of the 548 stays in a cell, for life, with parole explicitly off the table. The only thing any of them was spared is the execution chamber, in a state that has not executed anyone since 2006 and has had a moratorium on executions since Newsom imposed one in 2019.

“Clemency sounds like somebody walking out into the sunshine,” said Dale Krebbs, 54, a local man who reads commutation orders in full. “I've read this one. The sunshine is not in it. Life without parole is the sentence you get when the state wants to be absolutely sure you die inside, just on its own schedule instead of yours. If that's mercy, it's the kind where the recipient would not notice the difference without a press release.”

Then there is the granting itself, which is, for most of the 548, still pending. Newsom can commute 166 of the sentences on his own authority. The other 382 — nearly 70% — belong to people convicted of more than one felony, and under the California Constitution a governor cannot grant them clemency without a recommendation from a majority of the California Supreme Court. Newsom has asked the court to recommend a path by the end of the year, after which he would still have to sign individual orders. If the court does not act before he leaves office, the requests do not roll over to the next governor. As of Tuesday, then, the accurate verb for 382 of the 548 is not “granted.” It is “requested.”

None of this makes the underlying decision small, and this newspaper takes no position on whether commuting every death sentence in California is wise or wicked — victims' families were on both sides of it on the day it was announced, in public, by name. It is, by the Death Penalty Information Center's count, the largest mass commutation in American history, and reasonable people can call that monstrous all by itself. It merely observes that the decision being argued about should be the one that was made: a 19-day-old announcement, still mostly awaiting a court, that changes how 548 condemned prisoners will die — in prison, of old age, serving life without parole — and frees exactly none of them.

At press time, the 548 monsters had been located in their cells, where the clemency found them and where, under its terms, every one of them will remain for the rest of his or her natural life, and the mercy had been returned to the order, where it continues to be spelled l-i-f-e w-i-t-h-o-u-t p-a-r-o-l-e.

Actually True

Nobody was released, and nobody can be paroled. Gov. Gavin Newsom announced on November 5, 2026 that he would commute all of California's death sentences to life in prison without the possibility of parole, affecting 548 people (529 men and 19 women, per the governor's office). A commutation changes the sentence; it does not pardon the crime or open the prison door.

It was not “just days ago.” The announcement was made Nov. 5, 2026 — 19 days before the White House post of Nov. 24. Newsom said the action does not repeal California's death penalty and applies only to current death sentences; he has had a moratorium on executions in place since 2019, and California last executed a prisoner in 2006.

382 of the 548 have not been finally granted at all. Newsom can grant a blanket commutation to 166 inmates on his own. The other 382 were convicted of more than one felony, and the California Constitution requires approval/recommendation from a majority of the California Supreme Court first — nearly 70% of the total. Newsom asked the court to recommend a path by the end of the year so he can sign individual orders before his term ends; if the court does not act in time, the requests do not carry over to the next governor.

What is true in the original post: the number 548 is correct, the people on California's death row include murderers convicted of horrific crimes, and the scale is historic — the Death Penalty Information Center calls it the largest mass clemency/commutation in U.S. history, more than doubling the total number of clemencies granted in capital cases in the last 50 years.
