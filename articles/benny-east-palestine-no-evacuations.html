No Federal Agencies, No Alerts, and No Evacuations in East Palestine, Commentator Announces; the Record Shows the EPA Mobilized That Night, Troopers Went Door to Door, and an Evacuation Order Stood for Four Days

By Chip Hollister November 25, 2026 5 min read
🚂

WASHINGTON — The 2023 chemical detonation in East Palestine, Ohio, a commentator announced Wednesday, featured "No Federal agencies on ground," "No alerts to residents," and "No evacuations" — a hat trick of absence that the contemporaneous record, reached for comment, reports it cannot reproduce, having been present at the time.

"Trump in Iowa vs. Biden in Ohio," wrote Benny Johnson, introducing the comparison with today's Army detonation in Middletown, Iowa. "2023 Chemical Detonation in East Palestine: -No Federal agencies on ground -No alerts to residents - No evacuations -Deadly black chemical mushroom cloud -Tried to bribe residents to stay quiet -Toxic vinyl chloride and butyl acrylate found."

Begin with the derailment, because there was one, and it deserves to be met on its own terms. At 8:55 p.m. on February 3, 2023, 38 cars of a Norfolk Southern freight train came off the track in East Palestine, a village of about 4,700 people, and caught fire. What happened next is unusually well documented, because everyone involved later testified about it under oath. Governor Mike DeWine's Senate testimony records that less than an hour after the derailment, the East Palestine police asked the Ohio Highway Patrol for help, and troopers "quickly began going door-to-door with local police and Sheriff Deputies to check on residents and notify them of the one-mile evacuation zone." That is the alert — not a push notification, admittedly, but a state trooper on the porch is generally considered a notification method. The Ohio EPA's emergency response arrived at 12:25 a.m. on February 4 and began containing chemicals already in the creek. The U.S. EPA, per its own action memorandum, mobilized its Superfund response team at about 11:30 p.m. on February 3 — the same night — and had an on-scene coordinator in place by roughly 2 a.m., monitoring the air for, among other things, vinyl chloride and butyl acrylate. The NTSB was on scene gathering evidence that weekend as well. "No Federal agencies on ground" survives contact with this record for approximately zero of the derailment's first five hours.

The evacuations had the same problem, which is that they occurred. The village enacted an evacuation order on February 4. On February 5, with a tank car's temperature climbing and an explosion feared, DeWine activated the Ohio National Guard, ordered the evacuation of everyone within a mile, and put the rest of the town under shelter-in-place; troopers knocked on every door in the zone. On February 6 the zone was widened and officials went door to door again, this time removing people, before crews vented and burned five vinyl chloride cars in a controlled release — producing, it must be said, the deadly black chemical mushroom cloud, the one item on Johnson's list that checks out exactly as written, along with the chemicals themselves, which were indeed on the train. The evacuation order was lifted on February 8, which is a thing that can only be done to an evacuation order that exists.

Then there is the bribe. "Tried to bribe residents to stay quiet" names no briber, and the record offers only one candidate pool of payments: Norfolk Southern's. The railroad — a private company, and the party that had just derailed — paid residents a $1,000-per-person "inconvenience" fee, a $1,000 relocation allowance, and dollar-for-dollar reimbursement for hotels, food, and clothing. The IRS later classified these as qualified disaster relief payments, tax-free. No condition of silence was attached to them in any document anyone has produced; residents took the money and then, in large numbers, sued, testified, and talked to every camera in Ohio, which is not how successful bribery usually concludes. (A year later, in the $600 million class settlement, residents could take an additional payment in exchange for waiving personal-injury claims — a standard legal release, negotiated by lawyers, approved by a court, and upheld by the Sixth Circuit this month. A settlement release is many things, but it is a contract, not a bribe, and it arrived in 2024, not during the response the post is describing.)

"We ran that list against the log," said Marla Keene, 58, a fictional former county dispatcher who has kept the incident binder for three years out of professional habit. "Federal agencies: logged. Alerts: I helped send them. Evacuations: I have the door-knock sheets. The only line I can't find in the binder is the one where nobody did anything. Somebody has been reading a different binder. Mine has signatures."

None of this requires anyone to declare the East Palestine response a triumph — residents' anger at Norfolk Southern and at officials was real, the vent-and-burn itself was later found by the NTSB to have been unnecessary, and the town's grievances filled hearing rooms for years. It merely observes the distance between criticizing a response and deleting it. A response can be faulted for being slow, confused, or inadequate only if it happened. Johnson's version needs it never to have happened at all, so that today's Iowa response can stand alone in the frame. The troopers, the EPA teams, the National Guard, and the four days of evacuation orders were unavailable for the comparison, having been edited out of it.

At press time, the evacuation order lifted on February 8, 2023, could not be reached for comment on how it had been lifted if it had never been issued, and the door-to-door alert — a trooper, a porch, a resident — was still in the Senate record, being an alert.

Actually True

The derailment and the chemicals are real. On February 3, 2023, at 8:55 p.m., 38 cars of a Norfolk Southern train derailed and burned in East Palestine, Ohio (population ~4,700). The train carried vinyl chloride and butyl acrylate, among other hazardous materials (EPA action memorandum; Gov. DeWine's Senate Commerce Committee testimony, March 23, 2023).

Federal agencies were on the ground within hours. The U.S. EPA mobilized its Superfund Technical Assessment and Response Team at ~11:30 p.m. on February 3 and had an on-scene coordinator at the site by ~2:00 a.m. on February 4, beginning air monitoring; Ohio EPA emergency response arrived at 12:25 a.m. on February 4; the NTSB was on scene that weekend (EPA action memorandum; DeWine testimony; AP/CNN timeline).

Residents were alerted — door to door. Less than an hour after the derailment, Ohio Highway Patrol troopers, local police, and sheriff's deputies began going door to door notifying residents of the one-mile evacuation zone, and did so again on February 5–6 (DeWine testimony).

There were evacuations, ordered and enforced. The village enacted an evacuation order on February 4; on February 5 DeWine activated the Ohio National Guard and ordered evacuation within a one-mile radius, with shelter-in-place for the rest of the town; the zone was expanded on February 6 ahead of the controlled vent-and-burn; the order was lifted February 8 (EPA memorandum; DeWine testimony; contemporaneous timelines).

The black plume is real. On February 6, crews conducted a controlled vent-and-burn of five vinyl chloride tank cars to avert a catastrophic explosion, producing the black cloud in the famous photographs. The NTSB later concluded the vent-and-burn was not necessary — a legitimate criticism of the response, and a different one from "no response."

No "bribe to stay quiet" appears in the record. The 2023 payments were Norfolk Southern's (the railroad's, not the federal government's): $1,000-per-person "inconvenience" payments, $1,000 relocation allowances, and expense reimbursements, later classified by the IRS as tax-free qualified disaster relief payments, with no silence condition documented (Ohio House / Columbiana County JFS guidance; IRS, June 5, 2024). The 2024 $600 million class settlement offered an optional extra payment in exchange for waiving personal-injury claims — a court-approved legal release, upheld by the Sixth Circuit on November 10, 2026 — not a 2023 bribe.

What is true in the original post: the derailment, the controlled detonation and its black plume, and the presence of vinyl chloride and butyl acrylate. The false parts are the three "no"s and the bribe — which are the parts that make the post a comparison.
